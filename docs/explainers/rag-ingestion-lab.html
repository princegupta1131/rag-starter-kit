    Why?

    The search and the AI understand only words. Symbols like ## or Word's hidden XML are noise. The page number lets an answer say "see page 3".

    The science, simply

    No AI here, just careful reading. One exception: a picture is described in words by a vision model, so a chart or a photo becomes searchable text.

    In the kit

    ingestion_service/steps/step1_extract.py reads PDF, Word, TXT, Markdown, CSV, JSON and HTML. Images: step2_caption.py.
    Go deeper: what can go wrong

    Bad text in means wrong answers out. Scanned PDFs are pictures of text and need OCR first, which the kit doesn't include. Some Hindi PDFs store letters in a scrambled order and need a Word copy instead. In the app, open a document's View chunks to check what was really extracted.

    Why?

    One summary of a whole manual would be a blur of every topic. Small cards let the search hand the AI exactly the paragraph about leave.

    The science, simply

    Smaller card = sharper meaning, less context. Bigger card = more context, mixed topics. The overlap (amber) repeats the end of the previous card, so a sentence at the border is never lost.

    In the kit

    step3_chunk.py. In backend/.env: CHUNK_SIZE_WORDS=200, CHUNK_OVERLAP_WORDS=40. A sentence is never cut in half.
    Go deeper: exactly how the kit cuts

    1. Split the text into sentences. 2. Add whole sentences to a card until the next one would pass 200 words. 3. Start the next card with the last sentences of the previous one, up to 40 words. 4. Skip exact duplicate cards, like repeated headers. With these rules the real handbook becomes 2 cards in the database, of 199 and 185 words (they also include the title and the "try asking" list that this demo leaves out).

    Why?

    People don't use the document's words. Coordinates for meaning let "holidays" find "unused days", even across languages.

    The science, simply

    A neural network trained on billions of examples: texts that mean the same were pulled closer, unrelated ones pushed apart, until closeness in numbers became closeness in meaning.

    In the kit

    step4_embed.py sends each card to Gemini's gemini-embedding-001. EMBED_DIM=768.
    Go deeper: inside the model

    1. Tokens: the text is split into word pieces the model knows (carri · ed · over · unused · days). 2. Attention: a transformer reads all the pieces together, so each word is understood in context: "leave" next to "annual" means time off, not "leave the room". 3. One vector: everything is pooled into one list of 768 numbers for the whole card.

    No single number means "leave". Meaning is spread over all 768, like a fingerprint.

    04
    Cosine similarity

    The arrow duel

    Each list of 768 numbers is an arrow. Two texts mean the same when their arrows point the same way. Can you guess the angle?

    Real scores, real angles
    Your arrow's score0.50

    Play all 4 rounds to unlock this discovery.

    Why?

    We need one number that says "how close is this card to the question?", so every card can be ranked and the best ones picked.

    The science, simply

    Cosine looks only at direction, not length. Drag your arrow longer or shorter: the score doesn't move. A short question and a long paragraph can point the same way.

    In the kit

    Postgres does it: 1 - (embedding <=> question) in repositories/chunks.py. <=> is pgvector's cosine distance.
    Go deeper: the formula, with 4 numbers

    cosine = (A · B) / (|A| × |B|). Multiply the two lists number by number, add it all up, and divide by the two lengths:

    Real vectors do the same with 768 numbers. Gemini's vectors here aren't length 1 (this one is 0.59), and that's fine: cosine ignores length.

    Why?

    Everything in one row: words for the answer, numbers for meaning search, keywords for exact-word search. Normal SQL still works for filters and deletes.

    The science, simply

    The HNSW index links each point to its neighbours, like highways. A search starts on a few far-apart points and hops towards the question instead of checking everything.

    In the kit

    Table and index: backend/sql/migrations/001_init.sql. Saving: step5_store.py. See the rows yourself in pgAdmin (Setup Guide, Step 8).
    Go deeper: the SQL behind it
    -- turn on pgvector, store 768 numbers per card, and the keywords
CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS vector;
embedding  vector(768),
tsv        tsvector GENERATED ALWAYS AS (to_tsvector('english', content)) STORED

-- the index that makes meaning search fast
CREATE INDEX ON chunks USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops);

-- meaning search: the 20 cards closest to the question
SELECT content, 1 - (embedding <=> :question) AS similarity
FROM chunks ORDER BY embedding <=> :question LIMIT 20;

    HNSW is approximate: it can very rarely miss the single best match, which is why the kit fetches 20 candidates, not 1.

    What we have now

    Every card of the handbook is saved with its words, its meaning as 768 numbers, and its keywords. This happens once, at upload.
